Calibrating the learning rate

Fifteen knobs. One of them decides whether you learn at all. Here is how we measured it in about 35 seconds instead of 8 hours — by freezing the backbone and training only the head.

01 the knob

The knob that decides everything.

SinkSGD_ADV exposes about fifteen knobs: the learning rate, momentum, weight decay, the adaptive floor, warmup, the schedule shape, batch size, epochs, adapter rank, block size, the re-orthogonalization interval, and the usual regularizers. Most of them you can leave at their defaults and lose almost nothing.

One of them you cannot.

The learning rate sets the size of every step the head takes. Too small and fifteen epochs is not enough time to move; too large and each orthogonalized update undoes the last one. Everything downstream — the validation curve, the calibration, whether the run is worth resuming — is a consequence of this single number.

02 why the obvious loop is too slow

Measuring it honestly costs a working day.

The honest way to pick a learning rate is to train the real model at several values and keep the best one. Here is what that actually costs: DINOv3-B/16 at 512×512, batch 32, a measured 0.32 s per forward + backward step, 312 steps per epoch. Sweep five learning rates for fifteen epochs each and you are looking at a full working day per sweep.

—

The loop is not wrong. It is just priced wrong: you will not run it five times, let alone twenty, and a learning-rate sweep you only run once is not a sweep.

03 freeze what you are not tuning

Run the expensive thing once.

For a learning-rate sweep you are not tuning the backbone. You are tuning the head. So stop paying for the backbone on every step. Of the ~106M parameters in the model, 87M belong to the frozen DINOv3 trunk and only 19M belong to the head that trains.

The trunk's output is a deterministic function of the image. Run it forward once over all 10,000 training images, keep the 1,536-d features in bf16 — 10,000 × 1,536 × 2 bytes ≈ 30 MB — and from then on a training step is a matrix multiply against a cache that fits in RAM. Five learning rates finish in about 35 seconds.

Be precise about what this buys: the sweep measured a frozen-feature head, not the full model. The backbone never saw a gradient. That is exactly why it takes 35 s — and exactly why the winner still gets verified on the real run before it is trusted.

04 hold everything else still

A cheap surrogate is only useful if it is faithful.

Faithful here means one thing: between runs, the only thing that changes is the learning rate. Same 1,000 validation images. Same metric — micro-F1 at threshold 0.5 over the top-1000 tags, because the long tail of 12,476 labels has almost no support in 1,000 images and a full macro average would mostly be measuring noise. Same threshold, same seed, same schedule, same weight decay, same momentum.

Change two things at once and the five points stop being comparable: a better number no longer tells you which change produced it.

Plateau / sweet spot

—

Illustration — simulated loss vs. step, not a measured curve.

The five dots are the measurements. The line between them is interpolation. The sparkline on the right is a toy — it exists so you can feel what “too small / about right / too large” looks like, and it is labelled as an illustration everywhere it appears.

Read it left to right. Below 0.002 there is nothing measured at all. 0.002 and 0.005 are the same number to four decimals. By 0.01 you have lost two points of micro-F1; by 0.02 you have lost eight and macro-F1 has halved. The 0.05 point ticks back up — that is what five noisy points look like, not a second peak.

06 always compare against a known-good optimizer

A sweep tells you how hard the task is. A comparison tells you if you are broken.

An absolute micro-F1 of 0.39 means nothing on its own. For all you know, AdamW does much better on the same head and your optimizer is misconfigured. So run the reference: same frozen features, same protocol, same seed, different optimizer.

Hover a bar or a row. Click a row to pin it.

All rows are the same measured frozen-feature sweep with the identical evaluation protocol. Nothing in this table comes from a full-backbone run.

SinkSGD_ADV at 0.005 lands at 0.3924 micro / 0.1975 macro. AdamW at 0.003 — the stronger of its two checked points on micro-F1 — lands at 0.4025 / 0.1735. Micro-F1 is a point apart, inside what this eval can resolve; on macro-F1 SinkSGD is ahead. So the optimizer is fine. The ceiling is the task: 10,000 images, 12,476 labels, a 19M head reading frozen features.

The two ablations are the control. Weight decay at 0.01 costs about 0.006 micro; momentum at 0 costs about 0.008 micro and 0.03 macro. The defaults were fine. The learning rate was the knob that mattered — which is the entire reason we swept it instead of guessing.

07 pick the middle, then verify

Don't ship the argmax of a five-point sweep.

Freeze a backbone and the optimum sits slightly somewhere else than where it sits once the backbone is allowed to adapt. The surrogate cannot see that shift — by construction. So the sweep does not get to pick the number; it gets to pick the neighborhood.

—

0.005 wins the sweep over 0.002 by 0.0002 micro-F1. That is not a win; that is the resolution of the measurement. So we shipped 0.004: the middle of the plateau, a factor of 2.5 away from the cliff at 0.01, and the place where the real run's per-epoch validation curve has room to disagree in either direction without falling off anything.

How this transfers to any project

Nothing above is specific to SinkSGD_ADV, or to taggers, or to DINOv3. The recipe is: find the cheapest surrogate that is still faithful to the thing you are tuning, freeze everything else, score it with the exact protocol you will ship on, then sweep on a log scale. Any time the expensive part of your model is frozen for the question you are asking, the same trick applies — and the same trick applies to weight decay, momentum, and warmup length, not just the learning rate.

The part that transfers even further is the humility: the surrogate's optimum is not the model's optimum. Treat the sweep as a bracket, not as an answer, and let the real run's validation curve make the final call. And always run the boring reference optimizer on the same panel — because the number that matters is not “how good is my model”, it is “is my model worse than it should be”.
