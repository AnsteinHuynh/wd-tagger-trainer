Let me explain caching properly, because once it is clear the three possible designs separate cleanly and you can pick.

Caching means running the teacher over the corpus once, writing each image's output to disk, and then training the student from that file instead of from the images. During the student's training the teacher never runs again. Instead of a forward pass through SigLIP plus a decode of a tar member plus the teacher's adapters, you read 2 KB from a memory-mapped array. That is why it is attractive: the teacher's cost is paid once, roughly four hours, instead of once per step for however many epochs the student runs.

What I told you before and why: you cannot cache the OFT adapters. The adapters live inside the backbone at every attention and MLP module, so the embedding an image produces depends on the adapter weights. Caching the adapted model's output freezes those weights into a table — that is a teacher, not a trainable adapter. The part you can cache is whatever comes out after the last adapted layer, and only once the adapters are final. That is exactly why the order has to be: finish SigLIP plus its OFT first, then cache, then train the other model.

With that clear, there are three designs and they cost very different amounts.

| design | what is cached | size | what the student learns | when it can start |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| inference ensemble | nothing | 0 | nothing, the two models simply average their logits at scoring time | as soon as Dino plus OFT exists |
| soft-target distillation | the teacher's 12476 probabilities | 132.8 GB fp16 | the teacher's full output distribution, plus hard labels | after the cache pass, about four hours |
| feature alignment | the teacher's pooled embedding | 8.2 GB fp16 | a cosine or MSE pull toward the teacher's feature space | same |

The ensemble is the cheapest and needs no cache at all: train Dino plus OFT on hard labels exactly as we trained SigLIP, then at scoring average the two logits. Ensembles usually beat both members, and this one costs nothing but a second training run.

Distillation is the interesting one and it is what your proposal amounts to. The student's loss becomes the hard-label loss plus a term pulling it toward the teacher's probabilities. The reason it can help rather than just copy: soft targets carry the teacher's knowledge of which tags co-occur, so the student learns correlations it would otherwise take millions of images to see. The reason it might not: the teacher is a 20M-parameter adaptation trained on the same 5.32 million images with the same labels, so it has memorized them, and distilling from it hands the student the teacher's mistakes along with its knowledge.

My honest read is that the ensemble is the certain win and the distillation is the speculative one, and that we can have both — train the Dino model on hard labels first, score it alone and in ensemble with SigLIP, and only then decide whether spending 133 GB and four hours on a soft-target cache is worth a second student run.

Then let me teach all three properly, and add a fourth you did not ask for because it is free.

Ensembling. Each model outputs a score per tag; you combine them into one score and threshold at 0.40. The simplest combination is the arithmetic mean of the probabilities, and it works because two models make partly independent mistakes. If each has error variance s and the errors were completely independent, averaging two halves the variance and averaging k divides it by k. What actually happens is governed by the covariance: the gain is roughly s times (1 minus the correlation between members) over k. So the design rule is not "more members", it is "less correlated members". Two different backbones — DinoV3 and SigLIP2, different pretraining objectives, different data, different tokenizers of the image — are about as decorrelated as you can get, which is why your instinct is the right one. Two seeds of the same model are nearly the same model and will earn you very little.

Three ways to combine, and they differ. Average the probabilities, which is what people mean by ensembling and is robust. Average the logits, which is sharper and weights confident members more, but can be dominated by one overconfident member. Average the ranks, which throws away calibration entirely and is the safest when members disagree wildly. For us the practical detail that matters more than the choice: averaging shifts the score distribution, so the fixed 0.40 threshold no longer sits where it did for either member. Since the bar is scored at a fixed 0.40 and we are too, an ensemble has to be recalibrated on validation or its apparent gain will be eaten by the threshold. That is the single most common way ensembles show a gain in the lab and lose it on the test set.

Soft-target distillation. The student's loss becomes the hard-label loss plus a term pulling its distribution toward the teacher's, usually a KL divergence, often with a temperature that softens both distributions so the small probabilities matter. The reason it beats hard labels is that a one-hot label says "this tag yes, those tags no", while the teacher's distribution says "this tag yes, and among the noes these four are almost-yeses" — that structure encodes which tags co-occur, which is real information the student would otherwise need many more images to infer. The catch in our case is that the teacher has seen all 5.32 million images and memorized them, so its "soft structure" is partly its own overfitting. Distillation from a teacher trained on the same data works, but it transfers the teacher's confidence as well as its knowledge, and a teacher that over-predicts at 0.40 will teach the student to over-predict.

Feature alignment. Here the student is pulled toward the teacher's pooled embedding vector rather than its label distribution: minimise one minus the cosine similarity between the two vectors, usually through a small projection so the dimensions match. This transfers geometry rather than decisions, and it is the technique that helps most when the student's backbone is weaker — the teacher acts as a scaffold for the representation. Its weakness is exactly that it is not a decision: a student can sit close to the teacher in cosine and still threshold badly, because the two spaces only need to be linearly related, not identical, and nothing in the loss guarantees the relationship is the identity.

The fourth, which is free and I would take regardless. EMA, exponential moving average of the weights. You keep a shadow copy updated as a slow blend of the training weights and evaluate that copy. It costs one extra copy of 20M parameters, about 80 MB, and no extra compute, and it typically collects the same variance-reduction benefit as a small ensemble because the shadow is effectively an average of many nearby checkpoints. If you want an ensemble's benefit tonight rather than in two weeks, this is the one that does not need a second backbone.

For your case specifically, I would do it in this order. Train DinoV3 plus OFT on hard labels, because you need a second member before any ensemble exists. Add EMA to it, since it is nearly free. Then score three things on the same 50k from cached probabilities — SigLIP alone, Dino alone, the average — and recalibrate the blend weight and threshold on that same data with the bootstrap interval in hand, because a 0.01 gain in macro is one interval wide and you should not believe it without the interval.

One expectation to set now so the result does not disappoint: ensembling buys a few hundredths, not the 0.20 of macro that separates us from the bar. Your ensemble idea is right, and it is a real gain, and it is not a bridge to convnext. The bridge is still capacity in the Dino run.
