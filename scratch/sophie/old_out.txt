## How it works today

One tower, one head. `wdtag/model.py` loads DINOv3, wraps every `nn.Linear` in the backbone with `OFTLinear`, unfreezes the last N blocks, pools the token grid into a vector (768 for `cls`, 3072 for `cls_mean_max_std`), LayerNorms it, and puts one `Linear(pool, 12476)` on top. ASL sits on those logits. Three optimizer groups, one checkpoint, one validation path.

An ensemble adds a second branch to that picture and touches nothing else:

```
uint8 batch [B,3,512,512]
   |                       |
DINOv3 normalization   SigLIP normalization (mean/std 0.5, range -1..1)
   |                       |
12 blocks + OFT        24 blocks + OFT (optional)
   |                       |
pool -> 768 or 3072    MAP head -> 1024
   \                      /
      concat 1792 or 4096
             |
   Linear(concat, 12476) -> ASL
```

Concretely that is a second backbone in `DinoTagger`, `apply_oft_to_backbone` called twice (it is already generic), `adapter_state_dict` keyed by tower, five optimizer groups instead of three, and a head input of 1792 with cls pooling or 4096 with CMSS, so 22.4M or 51.1M.

## Your choice is a good one for a reason that is not the benchmark number

L/16 at 512 is 882,313,218 parameters total, of which the vision tower is about 303M: 24 blocks of width 1024, 1024 tokens at 512px. It is the strongest L variant in the family (INet 83.5 against 83.1 at 384), and more importantly it takes exactly the tensor our cache already holds. Same 512 squash, same token count as DINOv3, no second resize path, and both towers see identical geometry — which matters, because the geometry audit says our squash costs real accuracy and I would rather both towers pay it identically than have one see 384-cropped and one see 512-squashed.

## What OH-LT and unfreezing would cost on it

Here is the part I want you to see before we commit, because we just measured it on the DINO side.

OFT inside frozen blocks is not a forward-only proposition. A rotation in block 3 needs a gradient, so the backward pass still walks blocks 4 to 24 to deliver it, and every activation is retained for that walk. The evidence is arm u1, running now: dropping 11 unfrozen blocks bought 130 img/s against u12's 109, where the 4.25 img/s per block ladder predicted about 155. Roughly 25 img/s of the expected gain vanished into the backward the adapter forces.

Per image, at our measured anchor (3.53 ms per image for a 1024-token forward, 8.74 ms per image for a full u12 step):

| SigLIP-L/16-512 adaptation | extra cost per image | total step | img/s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| none, features precomputed | 0 in the loop | 8.74 ms | ~114 |
| forward only, in the loop, no adapter | +12.5 ms | 21.2 ms | 47 |
| last block unfrozen, no OFT | +13.1 ms | 21.8 ms | 46 |
| OFT everywhere plus last block | +16.9 ms | 25.6 ms | 39 |

Two things fall out of that column. The forward is 12.5 ms of which adaptation can never remove a cent, so once SigLIP is in the loop we are at 47 img/s and the full pass over 5.32M images goes from 15.5 h to 31 h. And OFT across its 24 layers adds another 3.7 ms per image on top, for a mechanism whose benefit nobody has measured on this tower.

Which leads to the design rule I would apply: a frozen tower with no adapter contributes only a deterministic function of the image, so running it inside the training loop buys nothing that precomputing it does not. Same math, no VRAM, no 12.5 ms. So

either we adapt SigLIP, and pay 3.5x per image for it, or we precompute its 1024-d vectors once and pay 12.5 ms per image once, which is 18.5 h for the corpus and 12.5 minutes for the probe.

There is a cheap middle that I think is the real answer if you want adaptation: unfreeze only its last block and put OFT on that block's own linears. Then the backward is one block, not twenty-four, and the cost is ~13.1 ms per image instead of 16.9. That is 28% of the adaptation cost for what is usually most of the adaptation benefit, since the last block is where the features that matter for a linear head live.

## The memory wall matters more than the speed

Our u12 run holds 20.4 GB of 24.5 at batch 48, and u1 holds 16.8. SigLIP-L retains activations for 24 layers at 1024 tokens and width 1024, roughly 2.7x the per-image activation of DINOv3's 12 layers at 768. At batch 48 that does not fit; the first thing the ensemble does is force batch 16 or 24, and smaller batches make everything slower than this table says.

Download L/16-512 — it is 3.6 GB of fp32 weights and the right pick regardless of design, because it is the variant that shares our geometry.

Then the probe, which is 12.5 minutes of GPU and needs no trainer change: extract its 1024-d features for the 10k train cache and the 50k val, fit the same ASL head, and score at 0.40 against DINO alone and against the concatenation. That single number tells us whether SigLIP's features carry tag information our tower lacks. One correction first, because it matters: with both towers frozen and no adapter inside either, both are cacheable. The blocker was never the tower, it is the adapter. Put OFT inside DINOv3 and DINOv3 becomes uncacheable while SigLIP2, still clean, stays cacheable. That is the "one but not the other" case, and it comes from OFT, not from the tower.

So yes, the picture you describe is right: one forward pass per image, one pooled vector per tower, 768 from DINOv3's cls and 768 from SigLIP2's MAP head, concatenated to 1536 floats, and training becomes a function on that vector. 5.32M images of that is 16.3 GB on disk, fp16.

## But rotating the embedding does not do the same thing as OFT

It is cacheable, yes, and it is also nearly pointless, for a reason worth seeing once.

A rotation applied after pooling is just a linear map on the 768-vector, and a linear map followed by a linear classifier is a linear classifier. The head learns W, the rotation applies R, and W times R is some other matrix the head could have learned directly, with no orthogonal constraint and no extra parameters. So a post-pool rotation can only help if something nonlinear sits between it and the classifier. Put a bottleneck there and it becomes a genuine low-rank adapter on the features, which is a fine idea, but it is a head-architecture idea and it is not OFT.

OFT inside the tower is a different function entirely, because the rotation is applied to every token at every layer, before attention. The queries and keys are rotated, so the attention pattern itself changes; the MLP inputs change; the residual stream carries a different signal into the next block. That is a change of representation, and it cannot be reconstructed from the pooled output, because the pooled output is the summary after all of that has already happened. One vector cannot carry the token grid.

There is a variant that sounds like it should work and does not: caching the token grid at the input to the last block, so the last block stays trainable. The grid for one image at 1024 tokens by 768 dims in fp16 is 1.5 MB, which is 8 TB for the corpus. That is why nobody does intermediate caching.

## What survives, once you cache

| trainable thing | cacheable | what it can express |
|---|---|---|
| linear or MLP head on 1536 | yes | arbitrary mixing of the two towers' summaries |
| bottleneck adapter after pooling | yes | low-rank reweighting of the summaries |
| learned per-tower gate or attention | yes | how much to trust each tower, per tag |
| loss, optimizer, threshold, schedule | yes | everything outside the model |
| OFT, unfrozen blocks, any rotation inside | no | a different representation |

The top four rows are not nothing. Our entire recent progress came from the loss and the head, and the probe suggests the head was undertrained in both cases. What caches away is the part we have never shown to be the bottleneck.

## The version that gets more out of the cache

One vector per tower is a choice, not a limit. We could cache the whole CMSS set, cls plus mean plus max plus std, which is 3072 dims per tower and 65 GB for the corpus at fp16, and give the head 6144-d inputs. Disk is not the constraint at our scale. The mean and std over the token grid carry a little of the spatial information that a single cls vector throws away, and it costs nothing at training time.

So the honest statement of what a feature-bank run would answer is this: how much of the gap closes when the representation is frozen and the head is given real capacity, our loss, our optimizer and the full corpus. If the answer is most of it, we have been paying 11.4 hours per pass for a fraction of a point. If the answer is little, then the representation is the bottleneck, OFT and unfreezing are earning their cost, and the two-tower trainer is worth its 23 hours.

Either way the extraction is a one-time 10 hours and it is not wasted: the vectors are also what the two-tower trainer would want if we go that way.
