## 1. Your RAM — it was a genuine leak, not perception

It really was growing forever. Diagnosis:

- Accessing Windows counters showed **4 worker processes at 9.01 GB private each** plus the trainer at 16 GB, and **commit 92.4 / 104 GB**.
- Cause A: `torch.load(..., mmap=True)` does not protect you from pickling. The debug-pic dump touches `ds_train.shards` in the parent *before* the next epoch spawns workers, so on Windows' spawn method each worker received the dataset via pickle — and pickling a mapped tensor serialises its **contents**. Every worker got its own full 7.9 GB copy of the train cache.
- Cause B: each epoch created a fresh DataLoader, so that copy happened again every epoch → stepwise growth. On top of that I opened the same shards 3–4 times (priors, dataset, targets), each a separate mapping.

Fixes: one process-level shared mapping per shard file (`open_shard`), a `__getstate__` guard that ships paths instead of tensors, `num_workers=0` (loading is memmap reads, it was never the bottleneck), and image normalisation moved to the GPU in BF16 instead of CPU float32.

Result: **commit 92 → 55 GB, private flat at 22.8 GB across epochs (it was 22.75 → 22.77 over 90 s)**, throughput unchanged at ~80 img/s.

## 1. The resume — fixed

Measured: **8.4 s → 3.0 s**.

There were two separate problems:

- **`_iter_tar` read every image blob while skipping.** It called `extractfile().read()` on each image *before* checking whether it was past the skip point, so resuming at step 50k pulled a full tar's worth of image data (~1.5 GB) through the drive just to throw it away. Now it tracks skipped images by filename only. That's the 8.4 → 3.0 s.
- **The count table lived in `I:\.cache\wd-tagger-trainer\manifest_train.json`** — and your own AGENTS.md says that tree is disposable. It only had **97 of 213** tars counted (it learns them lazily, ~10 s each), so a cache wipe followed by a resume near the end of the run would have cost **~19 minutes of header scanning**. I've moved it to `runs/_manifests/` (durable, survives cache clears) and a background job is counting all 213 train + 12 valid + 12 test tars right now, so that path is never paid again.

## 2. Compile — agreed, and the rule

The cache key is a hash of the *generated graph* plus the mode. Unfreezing changes the backward graph completely, so it's a genuinely different key — nothing to be clever about. Practical rule: `default` for anything exploratory (~1 min), `max-autotune` only for a long run where the one-time sweep amortises.

## 3. Separate LRs — you already have three

`lr_oft`, `lr_unfreeze`, and `lr_head` are already three independent param groups. The printed `lr 2.96e-04/1.77e-05/7.05e-05` is exactly that. What you *don't* have is a **sweep** of the backbone LR, which is the genuinely untested knob — and the drift data says we're moving fast (11% in 20k steps, `o_proj` at 40%). A warm-restart second pass starts at ~1.6e-5 naturally, slightly gentler than the 1.77e-5 we began this arm with.

Also worth being blunt about the "last-N with unfrozen OFT" idea: it's implementable, but unfreezing OFT *and* the base weights in the same layer is a redundant parameterisation (`R @ W` with `W` free spans every matrix), so the two parameters fight over the same effect. ## Validation caching — done, and it was needed

We had **only** the 1,000-image val cache (786 MB, 2 `.pt` shards). The 50k nested evaluation was re-streaming and re-decoding 50,000 images from the HDD tars every single run — about 4 minutes each time, competing with training.

Now built: a **nested 50,000-image validation cache**, 39 GB on `I:`, as a **`.npy` memmap** rather than `.pt` shards — deliberately, because `CacheDataset` loads shards fully into RAM and 39 GB would not fit beside a model in 48 GB. The memmap is read a batch at a time off NVMe.

**Nesting is by construction** (they're prefixes of one array), and I verified the sample isn't biased by the tar ordering:

| subset | supported labels | ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 4,669 | 0.374 |
| 5,000 | 8,973 | 0.719 |
| 10,000 | 10,809 | 0.866 |
| **50,000** | **12,454** | **0.998** |

Mean 32.3 positives/image — matches the expected 32.29. These ceilings are within noise of the shuffled sample I used earlier (4,621 / 10,863 / 0.998), so tar order is fine.

**One wrinkle you should know:** the trainer's in-run 1k val is *not* the same 1,000 images as this cache's prefix (only 84/625 matched). So from now on, **report numbers from the nested cache with the subset size stated**; the in-run val is monitoring only. That also explains the small offsets between figures I've quoted (e.g. 0.5264 vs 0.5365 at 25k).

Every checkpoint after the fork was missing its **72 block OFT rotations** — and reloading them didn't error, it just produced a broken model:

| checkpoint | reloaded | trainer's own val |
|---|---|---|
| 50,519 (fork start) | 0.5576 ✓ | — |
| 55,000 | 0.2236 ✗ | 0.5508 |
| 83,984 | 0.1945 ✗ | 0.5800 |

**Cause:** the fork froze the 72 rotations that 683cfec3 had trained (median 45°), and `adapter_state_dict()` saved only `requires_grad` parameters. Freezing preserves a rotation's value but removes it from the saved state. The live model kept them in memory, so training *and* in-run validation were correct — only reloading was broken, and it failed quietly.

**Impact if we hadn't caught it:** this run's checkpoints were unevaluable, unusable for an ensemble, and — worst — `--reset-optimizer` resume would have loaded a fine-tuned backbone paired with `R = I` and continued training from a broken state, silently.

**Recovered:** the fork never modified those rotations, so they're still in 683cfec3's step-50,519 checkpoint. Grafting them back gives **0.5788 @80k** and **0.5870 @83,984**, matching the trainer's own val exactly. All 7 checkpoints repaired.

**Fixed:** `adapter_state_dict()` now saves every parameter (110.2M vs 87M, ~370 MB — under 2× for a whole class of silent corruption gone). I first wrote a name-based predicate, then found it matched nothing because I was iterating `(name, param)` tuples — which is exactly the rot this kind of predicate invites, so I removed the predicate entirely.

## How it works today

One tower, one head. `wdtag/model.py` loads DINOv3, wraps every `nn.Linear` in the backbone with `OFTLinear`, unfreezes the last N blocks, pools the token grid into a vector (768 for `cls`, 3072 for `cls_mean_max_std`), LayerNorms it, and puts one `Linear(pool, 12476)` on top. ASL sits on those logits. Three optimizer groups, one checkpoint, one validation path.

An ensemble adds a second branch to that picture and touches nothing else:

```
uint8 batch [B,3,512,512]
   |                       |
DINOv3 normalization   SigLIP normalization (mean/std 0.5, range -1..1)
   |                       |
12 blocks + OFT        24 blocks + OFT (optional)
   |                       |
pool -> 768 or 3072    MAP head -> 1024
   \                      /
      concat 1792 or 4096
             |
   Linear(concat, 12476) -> ASL
```

Concretely that is a second backbone in `DinoTagger`, `apply_oft_to_backbone` called twice (it is already generic), `adapter_state_dict` keyed by tower, five optimizer groups instead of three, and a head input of 1792 with cls pooling or 4096 with CMSS, so 22.4M or 51.1M.

## But rotating the embedding does not do the same thing as OFT

It is cacheable, yes, and it is also nearly pointless, for a reason worth seeing once.

A rotation applied after pooling is just a linear map on the 768-vector, and a linear map followed by a linear classifier is a linear classifier. The head learns W, the rotation applies R, and W times R is some other matrix the head could have learned directly, with no orthogonal constraint and no extra parameters. So a post-pool rotation can only help if something nonlinear sits between it and the classifier. Put a bottleneck there and it becomes a genuine low-rank adapter on the features, which is a fine idea, but it is a head-architecture idea and it is not OFT.

OFT inside the tower is a different function entirely, because the rotation is applied to every token at every layer, before attention. The queries and keys are rotated, so the attention pattern itself changes; the MLP inputs change; the residual stream carries a different signal into the next block. That is a change of representation, and it cannot be reconstructed from the pooled output, because the pooled output is the summary after all of that has already happened. One vector cannot carry the token grid.

There is a variant that sounds like it should work and does not: caching the token grid at the input to the last block, so the last block stays trainable. The grid for one image at 1024 tokens by 768 dims in fp16 is 1.5 MB, which is 8 TB for the corpus. That is why nobody does intermediate caching.

Everything here runs on cached features, so each test costs minutes, not a training pass.

Let me explain caching properly, because once it is clear the three possible designs separate cleanly and you can pick.

Caching means running the teacher over the corpus once, writing each image's output to disk, and then training the student from that file instead of from the images. During the student's training the teacher never runs again. Instead of a forward pass through SigLIP plus a decode of a tar member plus the teacher's adapters, you read 2 KB from a memory-mapped array. That is why it is attractive: the teacher's cost is paid once, roughly four hours, instead of once per step for however many epochs the student runs.

What I told you before and why: you cannot cache the OFT adapters. The adapters live inside the backbone at every attention and MLP module, so the embedding an image produces depends on the adapter weights. Caching the adapted model's output freezes those weights into a table — that is a teacher, not a trainable adapter. The part you can cache is whatever comes out after the last adapted layer, and only once the adapters are final. That is exactly why the order has to be: finish SigLIP plus its OFT first, then cache, then train the other model.

With that clear, there are three designs and they cost very different amounts.

| design | what is cached | size | what the student learns | when it can start |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| inference ensemble | nothing | 0 | nothing, the two models simply average their logits at scoring time | as soon as Dino plus OFT exists |
| soft-target distillation | the teacher's 12476 probabilities | 132.8 GB fp16 | the teacher's full output distribution, plus hard labels | after the cache pass, about four hours |
| feature alignment | the teacher's pooled embedding | 8.2 GB fp16 | a cosine or MSE pull toward the teacher's feature space | same |

The ensemble is the cheapest and needs no cache at all: train Dino plus OFT on hard labels exactly as we trained SigLIP, then at scoring average the two logits. Ensembles usually beat both members, and this one costs nothing but a second training run.
